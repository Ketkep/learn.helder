# Ideas for daily editions

The nightly routine picks from this list, or finds something better that meets the bar in CLAUDE.md ("What makes an edition worth sharing"). Every number here is a lead to check, not a checked fact: research it before it goes on a page. Tick an idea off with its edition number when it is used, and add new ones at the bottom.

Each idea says: the hook, the mix-up most people have, what the reader does, the reveal, ideas for "Use it", a share line, sources to look for, and what to be careful with.

## Ready to research

### The page that reads your mind (the mind)
- Hook: press F and J as randomly as you can. The page guesses your next key before you press it, and it is right far more often than half the time.
- Mix-up: people think they can be random on purpose.
- The reader: types 100 keys. A live score shows the page's hits. A real coin, run by the page, scores about 50 per cent next to it.
- Reveal: what the page looked at. It remembered what you pressed after each run of four or five keys. People avoid long runs and switch too often.
- Use it: passwords people make up, rock paper scissors, a penalty taker's corner.
- Share: "The page predicted 63 per cent of my keys."
- Sources: Scott Aaronson's "oracle" program, studies of how people make up random sequences (reviews by Wagenaar, 1972, and Nickerson, 2002).
- Care: nothing leaves the browser, and the page says so.

### A traffic jam out of nothing (technology)
- Hook: put 22 cars on a circular road with no accident and no narrowing, and a jam still appears.
- Mix-up: every jam has a cause up ahead.
- The reader: watches a ring of cars, taps one car to make it brake for a moment, and sees a stop-and-go wave travel backwards. Then they change how closely drivers follow, and add one calm driver who keeps a gap.
- Reveal: the wave moves backwards while every car moves forwards. One calm driver can smooth it out.
- Use it: why you stop on a motorway for nothing, a queue at a supermarket door, people walking on stairs.
- Share: "One calm driver cleared my jam in 40 seconds."
- Sources: the Sugiyama experiment (New Journal of Physics, 2008), the Stern experiment with one automated car (2018), university pages on traffic waves.
- Care: a toy model of drivers, called a toy model on the page. Starts on a click, pauses, steps with reduced motion.

### 42 folds to the Moon (maths)
- Hook: a sheet of paper folded 42 times would be thicker than the distance to the Moon.
- Mix-up: doubling feels like adding.
- The reader: folds a sheet by dragging. The stack climbs past a book, a house, a mountain, the space station, the Moon, with the camera pulling back.
- Reveal: their guess of how many folds it takes, against 42. Then why real paper stops at about seven folds, and the record of twelve.
- Use it: the rice on a chessboard, a pond that fills with lily pads, a chain message.
- Share: "I guessed 1,000 folds. It takes 42."
- Sources: Britney Gallivan's folding formula and record (2002), NASA for the Moon's distance, the thickness of office paper.
- Care: the stacked paper is an idea, not a real object, and the page says so.

### Seven shuffles (games)
- Hook: a well shuffled deck is almost certainly in an order no deck has ever been in.
- Mix-up: shuffles repeat, and more shuffling is always better.
- The reader: riffles a deck on screen and watches the order spread out, then does eight perfect shuffles and sees the deck come back to where it started.
- Reveal: 52 factorial, written out, against the number of shuffles ever made. About seven riffles are enough to mix a deck well.
- Use it: playlists on shuffle, choosing a random winner, why a new deck needs more shuffles.
- Share: "Eight perfect shuffles and my deck came home."
- Sources: Bayer and Diaconis (1992) on seven shuffles, the number 52 factorial, faro shuffles returning after eight.
- Care: no betting or casino framing.

### Pick three flips, I pick better (games)
- Hook: you choose any pattern of three coin flips. The page always picks a pattern that turns up first more often than yours.
- Mix-up: every pattern of three is equally likely, so no pattern can beat another.
- The reader: plays rounds against the page with a coin flipped on screen, then sees the page's rule.
- Reveal: each pattern is equally likely, but which one comes first is a different question. Some odds are 7 to 1.
- Use it: waiting for a bus that comes in bunches, a song that repeats in a playlist.
- Share: "The page beat me 7 rounds to 3."
- Sources: Walter Penney (1969), Martin Gardner's columns, Humble and Nishiyama's card version (Significance, 2010).
- Care: a game between the reader and the page, no money, no betting.

### Why adding a road can slow everyone down (technology)
- Hook: a city added a shortcut and everyone's trip got slower. Remove a road and traffic can flow better.
- Mix-up: more roads always means less traffic.
- The reader: sends 4,000 drivers through a small network, opens and closes a new shortcut, and watches every driver choose the fastest route for themselves.
- Reveal: with the shortcut, everyone takes it, and the trip time goes up for all.
- Use it: a crowded lift, the fastest checkout queue, internet traffic.
- Share: "I closed a road and saved everyone 15 minutes."
- Sources: Braess (1968), the Cornell textbook by Easley and Kleinberg, reports on Seoul's Cheonggyecheon and New York's 42nd Street.
- Care: the network is a toy model. No claims about a real city's plans.

### Your friends are more popular than you (the mind)
- Hook: on average, your friends have more friends than you do. For most people, that is true.
- Mix-up: it must be bad luck, or just you.
- The reader: clicks people in a made-up town and compares each person's number of friends with the average of their friends.
- Reveal: popular people are counted in many friend lists, so they pull the average up.
- Use it: why the gym looks busy when you go, why your class seemed bigger than the average class, why your friends seem to travel more.
- Share: "In my town, 8 of 10 people had more popular friends."
- Sources: Scott Feld (American Journal of Sociology, 1991), the Facebook network study by Ugander and others (2011).
- Care: a made-up town, no real people, no data collected.

### When to stop looking (maths)
- Hook: you see flats one by one and must say yes or no on the spot. The best plan picks the very best flat about 37 times in 100.
- Mix-up: look at as many as you can, or take the first good one.
- The reader: plays rounds with hidden scores, decides when to stop, then lets the page run each plan 1,000 times.
- Reveal: look at the first 37 per cent without choosing, then take the first one better than all of those.
- Use it: when to buy a plane ticket, choosing a parking spot, hiring someone.
- Share: "I found the best flat 4 times out of 10."
- Sources: Thomas Ferguson, "Who solved the secretary problem?" (Statistical Science, 1989), Martin Gardner (1960).
- Care: a puzzle about chance, not advice for life choices, and the page says so.

### A flock with no leader (nature)
- Hook: no starling leads the flock. Three simple rules per bird make the whole cloud.
- Mix-up: someone must be steering.
- The reader: watches a flock on screen, switches each of the three rules off in turn (keep apart, line up, stay close), and chases the flock with the pointer as a falcon.
- Reveal: switch one rule off and the flock falls apart in a way you can predict.
- Use it: a crowd leaving a stadium, fish in a school, cars merging.
- Share: "I broke the flock with one switch."
- Sources: Craig Reynolds's "boids" (1987), the Rome starling study by Ballerini and others (PNAS, 2008), about seven neighbours per bird.
- Care: a simulation, called a toy model. Starts on a click and pauses.

### The first digit is usually 1 (maths)
- Hook: in many real lists of numbers, almost a third start with a 1, and fewer than one in twenty with a 9.
- Mix-up: every first digit should turn up equally often.
- The reader: looks at real lists built into the page (with sources), then makes up 30 "random" amounts and sees if theirs follows the pattern.
- Reveal: numbers that grow by multiplying spread evenly on a log scale, and that gives the pattern.
- Use it: why made-up figures can stand out, house numbers, prices.
- Share: "My made-up numbers failed the first digit test."
- Sources: Frank Benford (1938), Simon Newcomb (1881), Theodore Hill (1995), the source of every built-in list.
- Care: no claims that a real person or company cheated. The test is a hint, never proof.

### Earth's history as one year (history)
- Hook: if the Earth's history were squeezed into one year, people would show up in the last minutes of 31 December.
- Mix-up: dinosaurs and people feel close together in time.
- The reader: drags along a calendar year and lands on events, then guesses where the first animals, the dinosaurs and the first farmers go before the page places them.
- Reveal: their guesses against the real places, usually far too early.
- Use it: one human life on the same calendar, the age of the pyramids, the age of the Moon.
- Share: "I put the dinosaurs in June. They belong in December."
- Sources: the geological time scale (International Commission on Stratigraphy), museum pages, Carl Sagan's cosmic calendar for the idea.
- Care: dates with ranges where the sources give ranges.

### The sound that rises for ever (music)
- Hook: a tone that keeps going up and never gets higher.
- Mix-up: if a sound keeps rising, it must end up very high.
- The reader: builds the sound from layers an octave apart, and turns the soft edges of the layers on and off to break the effect.
- Reveal: the layers rise and fade in and out, so the ear keeps hearing a climb.
- Use it: a barber's pole, the never ending staircase drawing, film music that builds tension.
- Share: "I broke the endless staircase of sound."
- Sources: Roger Shepard (1964), Jean-Claude Risset.
- Care: sound carries this one, so the page also draws the layers, and it follows the sound switch. Keep the volume low by default.

### The coast that grows with your ruler (places)
- Hook: measure a coastline with a shorter ruler and it gets longer, without end.
- Mix-up: a coast has one true length.
- The reader: walks a pair of dividers along a coast at different steps and watches the total grow.
- Reveal: the bends inside bends. Then the same with a straight road, which does not grow.
- Use it: the length of a river, the size of a lung, a cauliflower.
- Share: "My coast was 2,800 km long, then 3,400."
- Sources: Lewis Fry Richardson's measurements, Benoit Mandelbrot, "How long is the coast of Britain?" (Science, 1967).
- Care: a drawn coast is a model unless its outline comes from a named public source.

### The four card puzzle most people fail (the mind)
- Hook: a puzzle with four cards that most people get wrong, until the same puzzle is about checking tickets.
- Mix-up: people think they test a rule by looking for cases that fit it.
- The reader: turns cards, then meets the same rule as a ticket check at a door.
- Reveal: only the cards that could break the rule matter.
- Use it: checking a claim in an advert, testing a theory, checking a rule at work.
- Share: "I failed the cards, then got the tickets right."
- Sources: Peter Wason (1966, 1968), Leda Cosmides (1989), the share of people who solve it.
- Care: not a test of anyone's intelligence, and the page says so.
